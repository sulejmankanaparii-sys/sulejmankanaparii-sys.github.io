A car insurance quote you can finish on your phone

Online car insurance quotes often mean a long form, unfamiliar terms and a price with no explanation. I designed a mobile quote flow for Kova, a fictional Ontario insurer. It shows an early estimate after three questions, explains each coverage in plain words, and passes your answers to a broker so you never repeat them.

The problem

A long form stands between people and a price

Insurers need a lot of information to price a policy. BrokerLink's guide says a basic quote needs your age, your location and your car's make, model and year. A more accurate one also needs your licence number, the car's VIN and your history of tickets and accidents.

Long forms lose people. In Baymard Institute's checkout research, 17% of US online shoppers said they had abandoned an order in the past quarter because the checkout was too long or complicated. That research is about shopping checkouts. The lesson about extra fields still applies to quote forms.

Choices also got harder in Ontario. As of July 2026, most statutory accident benefits are optional. Only medical, rehabilitation and attendant care benefits stay mandatory. More choice means more decisions for people who may not know the terms.

Problem statement

People who are new to car insurance in Ontario struggle to finish an online quote, because the form is long, the terms are unfamiliar and the price arrives without an explanation.

1 · Research

What I learned from desk research

No interviews in this round. Findings come from public sources, and assumptions are labelled.

A first price can come early

Age, location and the car are enough for a basic quote. The rest can come after the person sees a number.

Source: BrokerLink

One question per page is easier

Asking one question per page helps people understand what they need to do. When the question is also the heading, screen reader users hear it only once.

Source: GOV.UK Design System

Ontario coverage has new choices

From July 2026, most accident benefits are optional. People need plain explanations and a sensible default to choose well.

Source: FSRA

Accessibility is the law here

Ontario organizations with 50 or more employees must make public websites meet WCAG 2.0 Level AA. WCAG 2.2 adds rules that matter for long forms, like not asking for the same information twice.

Sources: Government of Ontario, W3C

2 · Define

How might we help a newcomer like Amira get a price she understands, on her phone, without repeating herself?

  • Price early, details laterShow an estimate after three answers, then refine it.
  • One question per screenThe question is the heading, so it is clear and read once by screen readers.
  • Plain words, explained in placeEvery insurance term has a short explanation next to it.
  • Help in the same place"Talk to a broker" sits in the same spot on every screen.
  • Never ask twiceAnswers follow the person to the broker and to a saved quote.

The flow

  1. Start
  2. Postal code
  3. Car
  4. Date of birth
  5. Early estimate
  6. Licence
  7. Experience
  8. Driving record
  9. Coverage
  10. Price
  11. Buy online or talk to a broker

From any screen, people can choose Talk to a broker or Save and finish later. Six short questions come before coverage, and the estimate appears after the third.

3 · Design

Key decisions

Each decision opens the matching screen in the live prototype at the top of this page.

Show an estimate after three answers

Problem
People answer many questions before they see any price.
Decision
After postal code, car and date of birth, a price range appears and stays visible while the person finishes.
Why
BrokerLink lists age, location and the car as enough for a basic quote.

Screen: Early estimate

One question per screen

Problem
Long pages of fields feel like work, and mistakes are hard to find.
Decision
Each screen asks one thing, and the question is both the heading and the label. Errors say how to fix them.
Why
GOV.UK Design System guidance on question pages.

Screen: Postal code

Count driving experience from abroad

Problem
Newcomers like Amira may not know that years of driving abroad can matter.
Decision
The experience question says to include years outside Canada and names the document to ask for: a letter of experience.
Why
BrokerLink's guide lists experience letters from outside the province as part of buying a policy.

Screen: Experience

Explain coverage where people choose it

Problem
Terms like DC-PD are confusing, and Ontario added new choices in July 2026.
Decision
Required coverage is already included, and each item has a "What's this?" explanation. Optional coverage shows its monthly cost, and the total updates as you choose.
Why
FSRA's standard policy page and the July 2026 change to accident benefits.

Screen: Coverage

Make the price readable

Problem
A single number gives no reason and no way to change it.
Decision
The price screen breaks the total into parts, shows what shapes it, and lets you trade a higher deductible for a lower price.
Why
The "Sees the price" pain point in the journey map.

Screen: Price

Help in one place, answers never repeated

Problem
Calling for help usually means starting over.
Decision
"Talk to a broker" is in the same place on every screen, and the broker receives every answer given so far.
Why
WCAG 2.2 criteria 3.2.6 Consistent Help and 3.3.7 Redundant Entry.

Screen: Broker call booked

4 · Accessibility

Accessibility built in from the first screen

A real version would need to meet WCAG 2.0 Level AA under Ontario's accessibility law (AODA). I designed for WCAG 2.2 Level AA, which builds on it. This table lists what the prototype does today.

WCAG 2.2 criterionWhat the prototype does
1.3.1Info and relationships
3.3.2Labels or instructions		Every input has a visible label, and the question is the screen heading. Groups of choices use a fieldset and legend.
3.3.1Error identification
3.3.3Error suggestion		Errors appear as text next to the field and in a summary at the top, and each one says how to fix it.
2.4.3Focus order
2.4.7Focus visible		Focus moves to the new question on each screen, and every control shows a clear outline.
2.5.8Target size (minimum)Buttons and choices are at least 48 px tall, twice the 24 px minimum.
2.4.11Focus not obscured (minimum)The sticky button bar never covers the field you are on.
3.2.6Consistent help"Talk to a broker" is in the same place on every screen.
3.3.7Redundant entryThe broker receives your answers, so you never type them twice.
3.3.8Accessible authentication (minimum)Save and finish later uses an email link. There is no password to remember.
1.4.3Contrast (minimum)Text has a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1.

Not yet tested with screen reader users. That is part of the test plan below.

5 · Validation plan

How I would test it

This design has not been tested with users yet. Here is the plan for the first round.

Who

Five people: two newcomers buying their first policy in Canada, two people renewing or switching insurers, and one screen reader user. Testing with five people finds most of the problems in a design, according to Nielsen Norman Group.

Tasks

  1. Get a price for this car.
  2. Find out what accident benefits cover.
  3. Lower your monthly price without removing required coverage.
  4. Ask a broker to call you tomorrow.

What I would measure

  • Task success and time on each task
  • Time to the first estimate
  • Errors on each screen
  • System Usability Scale (SUS) score

After launch, with analytics

  • Share of started quotes that reach a price
  • Drop-off on each screen
  • "What's this?" taps for each term
  • Broker calls booked and quotes saved for later

HypothesisIf people see an estimate after three answers, more of them will reach the final price.

HypothesisIf coverage terms are explained in place, fewer people will call with basic questions.

Next

What I would do next

  • Talk to real peopleInterview recent buyers and brokers to replace my assumptions with findings.
  • Run the first testTest with five people and fix the biggest problems first.
  • Check the estimateWork with pricing experts so the early range stays honest.
  • Build the componentsTurn the screens into reusable Figma components for a design system.

What I learned

Most of the work was deciding what not to ask yet. Moving the estimate earlier changed the order of every other question.

Accessibility shaped the design from the start. Rules like "don't ask twice" and "keep help in one place" made the flow simpler for everyone.
